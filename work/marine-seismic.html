Marine Seismic Deep Learning

Type
Team project, 3 people
Team
João Luiz Godoy, Lenny Lukach, Ryan Greene
My part
Horizon detection
Stack
Python · PyTorch · TorchVision · segyio · SciPy
Outcome
The seafloor horizon traced with a 5.6 m mean absolute error, 95.4% of predictions within ±15 m of expert picks.

A marine seismic survey images the rock under the seafloor by recording how acoustic pulses reflect off layer boundaries. Interpreting it means tracing horizons (layer boundaries) and faults (where layers break), and it’s usually done by hand by trained geophysicists. Our team built an end-to-end system that automates both.

Horizon detection (my part)

I built the horizon tool from scratch in PyTorch: a custom 2D CNN trained on the Netherlands F3 Block, a 3D volume of more than 600,000 traces with expert-picked seafloor horizons. It uses asymmetric pooling, compressing depth while keeping trace width, to match how a geophysicist scans a section by eye. It reaches a 5.6 m mean absolute error, with 95.4% of predictions within ±15 m of the expert picks. I also wrote its label generation, result visualization and a noise-robustness study.

Fault detection (the team’s pipeline)

The fault side is a three-stage pipeline. A classical detector fuses four fault-sensitive attributes into a probability map and traces faults with simulated annealing. Its output becomes COCO bounding boxes across dozens of survey lines, which removes manual labeling. A Faster R-CNN with a ResNet-50 and FPN backbone is trained on those labels and replicates the classical detector about 100× faster: 40 seconds for 203 images, against one to two hours per image. EigenCAM heatmaps show which regions it attends to.

Data

Faults come from 2D lines in the USGS National Archive of Marine Seismic Surveys; horizons from the Netherlands F3 Block, an open 3D survey from SEG. Both are stored as SEG-Y, read with segyio and ObsPy.
